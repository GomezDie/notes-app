It’s a freezing Thursday morning in Chicago.
February fourteenth, nineteen twenty-nine.
A black Cadillac turns onto North Clark Street.
It has a siren, a gong, and a rack for rifles.
To anyone watching, it’s a police car.
It stops outside a brick garage at number twenty-one twenty-two.
The sign out front says S M C Cartage Company.
But nobody here moves furniture.
This is a depot for the North Side Gang, the last crew in Chicago still standing in the way of one man.
Four men get out.
Two of them are wearing police uniforms.
Inside, seven men are waiting, reportedly for a delivery of hijacked whiskey.
When they see the uniforms, nobody panics.
In Prohibition Chicago, raids happen all the time.
Usually it means a fine.
Maybe a bribe.
So they do what they’re told.
They line up facing the back wall.
Then the officers open fire.
Two Thompson submachine guns.
Two shotguns.
Around seventy rounds in a matter of seconds.
When real police arrive, six men are dead.
One is still breathing.
Frank Gusenberg has fourteen bullets in him.
A detective asks him who did it.
“Nobody shot me,” he says.
Three hours later, he’s dead too.
The man everyone suspects is more than a thousand miles away, at his estate in Florida.
He has the perfect alibi.
His name is Alphonse Capone.
He’s thirty years old, and he’s the most famous gangster in America.
The massacre makes headlines around the world.
And it forces a question the government can no longer ignore: how do you catch a man who never pulls the trigger himself?
The answer will take a Treasury agent, a nervous bookkeeper, a few forgotten ledgers… and the most boring crime in the American legal code.
This is the story of the hunt for Al Capone.
